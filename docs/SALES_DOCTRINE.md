# TRU Sales Doctrine

**What TRU coaches real estate agents to do, and why.**

Read this before writing anything that judges, scores, labels or coaches an
agent conversation — a coaching brief, a pattern label, a prompt, a training
module, an email to a team. It is the source for all of it.

It exists because the alternative was inference. Eric would ask for something,
an agent would fill the gaps with generic sales-methodology instincts, and the
result would be wrong in a way that took a screenshot and a conversation to
surface. Everything below came out of interviewing him directly on 2026-08-25.
Where it does not cover a case, **ask** — do not pattern-match from general
sales knowledge, because general sales knowledge is what this document exists
to overrule.

Status of every claim here: stated by Eric, in his words where possible.
Anything marked OPEN is not yet decided and must not be guessed.

---

## 1. The one idea

Everything rests on **ASLAN Other-Centered® Selling**. Eric trained with ASLAN
in person and calls it the best training he has been through. It is the only
sales framework TRU is built on.

> Do not cite Maxwell's 5 Levels, Goldsmith FeedForward, or Stakeholder
> Centered Coaching as sales methodology. They were added to an earlier skill
> file at a time Eric describes as "before I fully understood AI," using names
> of respected organizations whose methods he does not actually know. They were
> removed on 2026-08-25. Do not reintroduce a framework Eric cannot speak to.

What ASLAN gets right, in Eric's account: it puts heavy emphasis on **the
psychology of the buyer** — how a human brain physically makes a decision, and
how to work *alongside* that process rather than against it. The goal is to
help someone decide without building an environment loaded with pressure.

### Drop the rope

The central image. A tug of war only has tension while both people pull. Name
the obvious thing that is creating pressure, out loud, and the tension goes.

Eric's own example, a first showing:

> "Hey guys, I just want to let you know that I am super excited to show you
> this house today. I know anytime you go out with a real estate agent,
> there's always maybe a little bit of pressure and you have to buy something.
> Guys, I just want you to know my goal is to make sure you guys find what you
> want, not what I want. And if that means we look at this house or 30 other
> houses over the course of the next couple of months while you make an
> informed decision about where you're going to live for the rest of your
> life, I'm going to darn well be here for you that whole step of the way. So
> please look at the houses, be critical, take your time, tell me what your
> concerns are, because my job is to help you find the right home for you, not
> the home that I think you should buy."

Note what that is and is not. It is not a softener bolted onto a close. It
names the buyer's actual unspoken fear, gives away the thing the agent wants
(this house, this trip), and means it. A "no pressure!" tacked onto a
follow-up text is not dropping the rope; it is a sign-off.

### What this does NOT mean

Other-centered is not passive, and vagueness is not kindness. Eric was asked
directly whether pushing for a committed time is the pressure ASLAN warns
about. His answer: **the flag is right, the framing is wrong.**

> Noticing that a conversation ended nowhere is fair. The coaching should be
> about why the buyer did not feel safe committing — not about technique for
> extracting a time.

So: still flag the conversation that went nowhere. Never coach it as
"be more insistent."

## 2. LEAD — the first-call sequence

TRU's own framework, Eric's, derived from Zillow's ALMS and preferred to it.
It has a real order, and the order has a purpose: **it keeps the agent out of
conversations they do not know how to handle and cannot recover from.**

| | Step | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **L** | **Lead with who you are** | Your name, your brokerage, that you are a featured partner with Zillow, and why you are calling. |
| **E** | **Extend the invitation** | Ask for the appointment. Give them an option between this or that. |
| **A** | **Ask and listen** | Permission first, then questions. Get to know them. Build rapport. |
| **D** | **Deliver the summary** | Give the call back to them. Confirm what is next. |

### The appointment goes early, and this is the single most load-bearing fact

The invitation is step **E** — second, near the top of the call. Not the close.

Zillow tracked this: asking for the appointment at the beginning of the call
produced an **80% increase in appointments occurring**. Eric's word for the
statistic is "indisputable."

Strength: **strong default, not absolute.** It is what TRU teaches and expects.
A good agent reading the moment may earn the right to wait. So: **flag it, do
not fail it.**

> Anything that coaches an agent to secure the time at the END of a
> conversation is teaching the opposite of LEAD. This was live in TRU HQ for
> one day (2026-08-25) and is the exact failure this document exists to stop.

### Where ALMS still shows through

ALMS: strong introduction, **A**ppointment asked for early, **L**ocation (where
are they looking, where are they coming from), **M**otivation (what is
prompting the move), **S**ummarize back to the buyer. Location and motivation
are the substance of LEAD's **A** step. Some of ALMS Eric considers outdated;
LEAD is the current framework and the one to coach against.

## 3. The early goal is the meeting — barriers are the enemy

Before an agent has met a buyer, almost nothing else matters. The skills that
win the client need a room to be used in.

So TRU **pushes back hard** on anything that puts friction between a buyer and
that first meeting — including questions that are perfectly legitimate later.

### Barrier questions

- "Are you pre-approved?"
- "Are you working with an agent?"

Eric on why these fail:

> It's often asked in such a poor fashion that the buyer is annoyed. They just
> want to see the house. They don't want to go through a crazy 15 minutes of
> questions. The questions feel like they're relevant to the agent, not
> relevant to the buyer.

And on financing specifically:

> A pre-approval is genuinely an important piece of the transaction. But
> talking about financing when somebody does not know you and all they said
> was they just want to see the home does not land well. It's a private topic.
> Most people do not actively talk about their finances with people they do
> not know.

The coaching point is never "don't qualify." It is **timing and framing**: this
is a private subject, raised by a stranger, at the moment the buyer wanted a
door opened.

## 4. Negative information has a technique

The failure Eric named as most costly, after giving up: an agent hits something
difficult and detonates the call.

The canonical case: **the property is already under contract.** The untrained
instinct is to lead with it — "just get it out of the way, that makes sense,
right?" — and the buyer is gone before a meeting was ever on the table.

Same shape: showing restrictions, a home out of their range, a listing that is
not what the photos suggested.

The pattern to coach: state the truth honestly, then immediately move to what
**is** still possible, and keep the meeting alive. Never the truth alone,
delivered and left sitting there.

> These agents are frequently not traditional salespeople and have had little
> to no sales training. Handling negative information is sales 101 that nobody
> ever taught them.

## 5. The three failures

Eric's own diagnosis of what actually loses clients. In his order.

### 1. They give up too quickly — and it is two separate problems

Both are real and must be named **separately**:

- **Abandoning the lead.** Attempts stop and the lead goes quiet.
- **Folding mid-conversation.** The live moment got difficult and the agent
  retreated instead of working through it. Turning "I just had questions" into
  a real meeting. Handling the under-contract property without losing them.

Quality outranks count: ten "just checking in" texts is not persistence.

**OPEN:** no numeric standard has been set for abandonment — how many real
attempts, over what window, before going quiet is a finding. Do not invent one.

### 2. They do not understand *why*

The root cause, and it has a direct product consequence.

> We might train on generic overviews of how to handle the first call... but it
> all falls apart the second they're put in a situation where you tell them,
> "Hey, you need to call first instead of text." And they're like, "Why? Why
> would I not do that? That doesn't make sense. Why would I not text them the
> properties under contract? Just get it out of the way."

**Therefore: a directive without its reason is worthless output.** Every
coaching point TRU produces must carry the reasoning a leader would need to
answer "why does that matter?" from an agent who has never been taught sales.
An instruction alone gets ignored, and reasonably so.

### 3. They frame everything around themselves

> A lot of these agents will not say this out loud. They could give a crap
> about the buyer's intentions. They care about selling a home, slamming a
> deal, getting them done.

How to handle this in output — the rule is precise:

- **Flag the behavior, not the motive**, on any single instance. Say what they
  did: raised the transaction before the person.
- **Once the ninety-day memory shows it repeating**, naming the pattern is
  fair. This is what the rolling memory is *for*. Eric's own phrasing of an
  acceptable call:

  > "Based on what I've seen, it is becoming more and more apparent that this
  > agent is far more concerned with checking their own boxes than they are
  > with the clients, as they have continued to repeat this exact same
  > pattern."

Never assert a motive from one conversation.

## 6. Who the output is written for

**Eric and the team leader, together.** Not the agent.

> This is for me and the team leader. It's writing for a team leader and myself
> who work in conjunction with them.

Team leaders see **everything Eric sees for their own team.** Not other teams'.
Nothing here is hidden from a leader about their own people — many of them are
actively coaching alongside Eric, and they need it.

Consequence for writing: the voice is one operator to another. Carry the *why*
(section 5.2), because the leader will have to answer it. Do not write it as a
message to forward to the agent.

## 7. Coaching categories, in TRU's language

The taxonomy Hermes sends. **The four `lead_*` keys are the LEAD steps** — this
was guessed wrong once already, on production, and cost a day.

| Key | What it means | Coach it as |
|---|---|---|
| `lead_l` | **L** — did not lead with who they are | Name, brokerage, Zillow featured partner, why you're calling |
| `lead_e` | **E** — did not extend the invitation | Ask for the appointment early, this-or-that. NOT "pin them down at the end" |
| `lead_a` | **A** — did not ask and listen | Permission first, then real questions. Rapport before transaction |
| `lead_d` | **D** — did not deliver the summary | Hand the call back, confirm what's next |
| `call_first` | Texted where a call was needed | Always carry the why — this is the directive agents most often reject |
| `call_quality` | Call ended before a conversation started | Worth coaching on its own; cannot support claims about technique |
| `next_steps` | Conversation ended nowhere | Frame as why they didn't feel safe committing, never as extraction technique |
| `objection` | Let an objection end the thread | This is "folding mid-conversation" (5.1) |
| `text_transition` | Sent details before talking | Ties to the barrier rule and to getting to a live conversation |
| `negative_property_pivot` | Left the bad news sitting there | Section 4. Truth, then what's still possible, meeting intact |
| `premature_financing` | Raised money too early | Section 3. Private topic, stranger, they wanted a door opened |
| `premature_representation` | Promised what they can only ask for | Also covers "are you working with an agent?" as a barrier |

An unrecognised key must render in its own words rather than be dropped —
Hermes owns this taxonomy and can extend it.

## 8. Rules for anything that produces coaching output

1. **Never coach securing the time at the end.** The invitation is step E.
2. **Always carry the why.** An instruction without a reason will be ignored.
3. **Behavior, not motive** — until the ninety-day memory earns it.
4. **A claim needs evidence that can support it.** A one-line call summary
   cannot prove what an agent did *not* say. Absence from a summary is not
   evidence of absence. (Live problem: 44 of 58 call quotes are FUB summaries,
   not speech. Flagged to Hermes 2026-08-25.)
5. **No guessed pronouns.** The roster does not record them. Use "them".
6. **Leaders and agents are different people.** Team leads, brokers and office
   staff are analysed but not coached in agent-facing output.
7. **Say it once.** A habit that has not moved since yesterday is still true
   and still in the app; repeating it daily trains people to stop reading.
